5 Bookkeeping Mistakes That Cost Small Businesses Money

By Brittany Nation, bookkeeper. A content writing sample for a small business audience.

Most bookkeeping mistakes are not dramatic. Nobody empties the business account or forgets to file a year of taxes. The money leaks out quietly: a fee here, a missed deduction there, a penalty nobody saw coming. The good news is that the same five mistakes show up again and again, and every one of them has a practical fix. Here they are, plus what to do about each.

1. Mixing personal and business money

It starts small. The business card is at home, so a supply run goes on a personal card. A deposit lands in the wrong account and never gets moved. By tax time, the two are tangled together, and sorting them out costs hours of bookkeeping time at exactly the moment you can least afford it.

The cost is real. Commingled funds can weaken the legal protection your business structure is supposed to give you, and every mixed transaction has to be explained, categorized, and documented. Meals, supplies, and mileage get missed, which means deductions get missed, which means you pay more tax than you owe.

The fix: Open a separate business checking account and run everything business through it, no exceptions. Pay yourself a regular transfer or paycheck instead of treating the business account like a wallet. If a personal purchase slips through once, reimburse it and note it the same week, not in December.

2. Having no receipt system

Receipts live in glove compartments, coat pockets, email inboxes, and the bottom of tool bags. When the IRS or a state agency asks you to prove a deduction, a bank statement line is often not enough. You need the receipt that shows what was bought, not just where and how much.

Without receipts, deductions get disallowed, sales tax audits get painful, and your bookkeeper spends billable time playing detective instead of keeping your books current.

The fix: Pick one capture habit and make it automatic. Snap a photo of every paper receipt the moment you get it, and store the photos in one folder or a receipt app. Set a weekly fifteen minute appointment with yourself to forward the email receipts to the same place. The system matters less than the habit.

3. Never reconciling your accounts

Plenty of owners check their bank balance in an app and call that bookkeeping. But the balance in your accounting software only tells the truth if it has been reconciled against the bank, meaning every transaction has been matched to the statement, line by line.

Unreconciled books hide duplicate entries, missed deposits, wrong amounts, and occasionally genuine bank errors or unauthorized charges. They also mean every report you pull, from profit and loss to cash flow, is built on numbers nobody has verified.

The fix: Reconcile every bank and credit card account once a month, right after the statement closes. It is a short job when it is monthly and a miserable one when it is yearly. If you are behind, start with the oldest month and work forward in order.

4. Guessing at payroll taxes

Payroll taxes are the one area where guessing gets expensive fast. Withholding the wrong amounts, depositing late, or treating employees like contractors can stack up penalties and interest that far exceed what doing it right would have cost. The IRS charges for late deposits even when the tax itself gets paid in full a few days behind schedule.

There is a cash trap here too. The money withheld from paychecks is not your money. Businesses that spend it during a slow month are borrowing from the tax agency at the worst possible rate.

The fix: Use a payroll service or software instead of calculating by hand. The cost is modest and it handles withholding, deposits, and filings on a schedule. Whatever your payroll total is, move the tax portion to a separate account the day payroll runs, so it is never sitting in operating cash looking spendable.

5. Never closing the month

Many small businesses have books that are technically entered but never finished. Invoices sit unsent, bills sit unentered, and the profit and loss statement gets looked at once a year, at tax time, when nothing can be changed anymore.

A monthly close is what turns bookkeeping from record keeping into information. It is how you notice that a subscription quietly doubled, that one job type loses money, or that a customer has not paid in ninety days while there is still time to act.

The fix: Build a short monthly close routine. Send every invoice, enter every bill, reconcile every account, then read three numbers: what you earned, what you spent, and what you are owed. Thirty to sixty minutes a month is enough for most small businesses, and it pays for itself the first time it catches something.

Start with one fix

You do not have to repair all five this week. Pick the one that made you wince, fix that one first, and let the habit build. Clean books are not about perfection. They are about knowing where your money is, and why.

If your books are behind, tangled, or just taking more of your evenings than they should, a bookkeeper can clean them up and keep them that way month to month. Start with a conversation about where things stand. The earlier the cleanup starts, the cheaper and easier it is.
